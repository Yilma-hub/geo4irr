# Geo4Irr — irrigation and nutrient decisions for Delaware

Irrigation and nitrogen decision support for Delaware cropland. Adapted from the
Geo4Irr architecture in the Arabi et al. project narrative (Colorado / New
Mexico) to Delmarva conditions, data sources, and regulation.

Open `frontend/index.html` in a browser to see the grower-facing app running
against the built-in engine. No server needed for that.

**Against the proposal:** `PROPOSAL_COMPLIANCE.md` maps every objective,
activity and equation in the narrative to where it is implemented and how it was
verified, and lists what is still missing.

**To test it:** `TESTING.md` is a step-by-step guide with the expected number for
every check — the automated suite, hand-verifiable engine values, every API
endpoint with its exact response, a manual walkthrough of the web app, and
sample boundary files generated by `tools/make_samples.py`.

---

## What it does

Three answers, in the order growers care about them:

1. **Do I start the pivot today?** A FAO-56 dual crop coefficient water balance,
   run daily from planting, against real weather.
2. **If not today, what day?** A 100-member ensemble of the same balance run
   forward over the National Weather Service forecast, with rainfall perturbed
   because that is the dominant uncertainty at a seven-day horizon on Delmarva.
3. **How much, and what does it cost?** Depth capped by what the profile can hold
   and what the system can deliver, converted to run hours and dollars.

And one question asked after the fact:

4. **How much water did the crop actually use?** A METRIC surface energy
   balance on Landsat 8 and 9, which measures what left the field from how hot
   the ground was rather than estimating what should have left it from a crop
   coefficient. Per-pixel maps at 30 m, weekly averages, and a running total
   from planting to any date, stopping at harvest. See *Satellite consumptive
   water use* below.

Plus two supporting tools: a nitrogen planner that credits poultry litter
properly and splits the rate for leaching-prone soils, and a sensor siting
designer that says how many probes to buy and where to put them.

## Why a Delaware version is different from the Colorado one

The source narrative is built for the South Platte and the Ogallala: deep
profiles, water scarcity as the binding constraint, deficit irrigation as the
strategy. Delaware inverts most of that.

| | Colorado / New Mexico | Delaware |
|---|---|---|
| Binding constraint | Water availability, allocations, aquifer decline | Soil water *storage*; nitrate leaching to shallow groundwater |
| Profile | Deep loams, 6–10 in of available water | Coastal Plain sands, 2–3 in in the root zone |
| Rainfall | 12–16 in/year, irrigation is the water supply | ~45 in/year, irrigation is drought insurance |
| Strategy | Deficit irrigation to cut consumptive use | Full irrigation, small frequent sets, avoid drainage |
| Nutrient issue | N and P export to surface water | Nitrate to the Columbia aquifer, Inland Bays, Nanticoke |
| Regulation | Water rights and transfers | Nutrient Management Law: certification and plans at 10+ acres |
| Mesonet | CoAgMET | DEOS, ~40 stations, University of Delaware |

Three consequences run all the way through the code:

**Storage, not supply, is the constraint.** Thirty inches of Evesboro loamy sand
holds about 2.1 inches of usable water. A scheduler tuned for a Midwest silt loam
will happily recommend a one-inch set that the profile physically cannot keep.
Every recommendation in `advisory.py` is capped at `min(depletion, 0.90 × TAW,
system max)` and the excess is reported as deep percolation, not silently
absorbed.

**Deficit irrigation is mostly the wrong advice here.** The narrative's core
research thrust is limited irrigation. In Delaware, with 45 inches of rain and
sands that cannot buffer a dry week, deliberately running a deficit on corn
during pollination is not a water conservation strategy, it is a yield loss. The
MAD logic tightens rather than relaxes in the critical window.

**Water and nitrogen are the same decision.** Deep percolation is the transport
pathway for nitrate. `nitrogen.py` connects them: an over-application shows up in
the interface as pounds of nitrogen lost, not only as a wasted hour of pumping.
This is also the strongest argument for the tool with DNREC, the Nutrient
Management Commission, and the Inland Bays program — it is a water quality
intervention that growers adopt for agronomic reasons.

**Shallow water tables are real and modelled.** Fallsington and Pocomoke sit over
a seasonal high water table that can feed a stressed root zone by capillary rise
in July. Ignoring it is why generic schedulers over-irrigate these fields.
`SoilProfile.upflux_in` models it as demand-driven exponential decay with the gap
to the water table.

```bash

cd geo4irr
open frontend/index.html          # macOS
# xdg-open frontend/index.html    # Linux
# start frontend\index.html       # Windows
```

the FAO-56 balance in the browser, and needs no server, no build step and no
account. The map and the soil lookup need an internet connection; everything else

```bash
cd backend
python3 -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate          # Windows: .venv\Scripts\activate
pip install -r requirements.txt
python -m pytest tests/ -q         # expect: 244 passed
```

geometry and the proposal methods are all behaving. Everything after this point

  `pip install "pyfao56 @ git+https://github.com/isukendall/pyfao56.git"`.

Open `frontend/index.html`, find this line near the top of the `<script>` block:

Set it to your API's origin. That switches the app from the built-in JavaScript

### Step 5 — Accounts (only if more than one person uses it)

Opened as a local file, Geo4Irr has no accounts and needs none: the data lives
in your browser, on your machine. Accounts exist for the API, and they are
enforced there — a password checked by JavaScript in a page anyone can read the
source of is not a lock.

Create the first administrator. There are no default credentials in this
codebase, deliberately:

```bash
cd backend
python -m app.create_admin        # prompts; nothing is echoed or logged
```

Or start the API, open the app with `API_BASE` pointing at it, and the first-run
screen does the same thing. Either way the setup route closes itself permanently
the moment an account exists.

Then, signed in as the administrator, use **Invite** in the header. Each
invitation is a single-use link that expires in 14 days; send it yourself, the
way you would send a key. Twenty seats by default — raise it with
`GEO4IRR_SEATS`.

| What | Where |
|---|---|
| Password storage | scrypt, per-user salt, 32 MB work factor. The password itself is never stored, logged, or recoverable |
| Sessions | random server-side tokens, HttpOnly + SameSite=Lax cookie, 12 hours, revocable |
| Deactivating someone | ends their live sessions immediately and frees the seat |
| Database | SQLite at `AUTH_DB` (default `geo4irr_auth.db`) |

If a password is lost it cannot be read back — that is the point of hashing it.
Delete the database file and run `create_admin` again.

## Setting up a field

A grower draws the outline on aerial imagery or uploads the file their monitor
already wrote — shapefile zip, KML, or GeoJSON. Everything else follows from the
shape: acres, the nearest DEOS station, and the soil. Nobody types a latitude.

File parsing happens in the browser (`unzip` uses `DecompressionStream`, and the
shapefile and KML readers are about 120 lines between them), which means boundary
setup works before anyone has an account and before the server is involved.

**Soil is resolved for the polygon, not the centroid.** This is the part that
matters agronomically. A 130-acre circle on the Coastal Plain routinely spans
Evesboro loamy sand and Fallsington sandy loam; one holds about 2.1 inches in a
30-inch root zone with no help from below, the other holds twice that and gets
capillary rise from a seasonal high water table in July. A point lookup picks one
of them at random and is wrong on half the field.

So `geo.ssurgo_for_boundary` samples a deterministic stratified grid of points
inside the boundary, tallies map units, and returns area-weighted components. It
samples rather than using a spatial intersection query because Soil Data Access
returns intersection *areas* only through the spatial query template, which has
changed shape across SDA versions and fails in ways a client cannot detect.
Twenty-four points put a third-of-a-field component inside about ten percentage
points — far tighter than the SSURGO lines themselves, and plenty to answer the
question being asked, which is whether the field needs splitting.

**When a field is genuinely mixed, it says so and offers a real choice.** Two
conditions are both required before recommending a split: no component covering
three quarters of the field, and at least 0.04 in/in of spread in water-holding
capacity. That threshold is roughly 1.2 inches across a 30-inch root zone, about
four days of corn in July — the point at which one schedule stops serving both
halves. Half Sassafras and half Ingleside is mixed on the map and identical in
the pipe; that gets no warning. Half Evesboro and half Matapeake is two different
farms.

The choice offered is honest about the tradeoff rather than picking for the
grower: schedule to the lighter ground and protect the acres that stress first
while over-watering the rest, or take the area-weighted average and serve neither
half well. On a shallow water table the first option is how nitrogen reaches the
ditch, and the interface says so. Fields that trip this test are the ones where
variable rate irrigation actually pays.

SSURGO is mapped at 1:12,000 or coarser and the lines are frequently off by a
hundred feet. Every lookup ends with the grower able to override it, because they
know where the sand blows and the survey does not.

## Satellite imagery

Which satellite does what, because the two get conflated constantly:

**Sentinel-2** is the optical one — thirteen bands from blue to shortwave
infrared at 10–20 m, revisit about five days. Every vegetation index comes from
here. **Sentinel-1** is radar. It has no red and no near-infrared, so it cannot
produce NDVI or a true-colour picture. What it does is see through cloud and at
night, which on Delmarva in July is not a footnote — a week of overcast blanks
the optical record entirely, and the radar keeps reporting.

Both are free through the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem. Register, create an
OAuth client under User Settings, and set `CDSE_CLIENT_ID` and
`CDSE_CLIENT_SECRET` on the API. The monthly processing allowance is far more
than field-scale work consumes. Credentials stay on the server: a client secret
handed to a browser is a client secret published, so the frontend asks the API
and the API asks Copernicus.

Eight layers, each clipped to the field outline so a grower sees their own ground
and not the neighbour's:

| Layer | What it's for |
|---|---|
| True colour | Blown nozzles, drowned corners, replant strips |
| Infrared | Vigour before canopy closes |
| **NDVI** | Vigour — and the layer that feeds the water balance |
| **NDRE** | Red edge; keeps separating good from poor after NDVI flattens |
| SAVI | NDVI corrected for bright sand showing through, best pre-V6 |
| GNDVI | Chlorophyll, so more sensitive to nitrogen |
| **NDMI / MSI** | Water held in the leaves — the optical read on stress |
| Radar (S1) | Backscatter; works through cloud |

Cloud masking is not optional. Scene Classification classes 3, 8, 9 and 10 are
cloud shadow and cloud, and they are dropped inside the evalscript before any
field mean is computed — a mean taken across a cloud edge is worse than no
number, because it looks like a number. Intervals where less than 55% of the
field was visible are discarded rather than reported.

**The point is not the picture.** The water balance carries a multiplicative bias
on the tabular crop coefficient, and the filter in `bayesian.py` has always been
able to correct that bias from a canopy observation — but nothing produced one.
Field-mean NDVI is that observation. A field that closed canopy two weeks early
now gets scheduled on what it is actually doing instead of a textbook curve, and
the screen says so in those terms: *"The canopy is ahead of the calendar. The
crop is using more water than the textbook curve assumes."*

The observation arrives with honest uncertainty attached. It widens with spatial
variability inside the field, and widens again above NDVI 0.78, because a
saturated index cannot tell a comfortable crop from a hurting one and must not be
allowed to overrule a water balance that knows better. That is also why NDRE is
offered: it keeps working where NDVI has stopped.

Every layer carries its own caution in the interface. NDMI and MSI are canopy
water, not soil water — they lag real stress by a few days and fall naturally
during dry-down, so a mid-season drop is a reason to check a probe, not a
measurement of depletion. Sentinel-1 is not calibrated soil moisture; read the
change between passes, not the absolute number.

One thing the imagery does that a soil map cannot: the spread *within* one scene
is the crop reporting its own variability, rather than a survey line predicting
it. When a high coefficient of variation persists across scenes and matches the
soil components under the field, that is the strongest case for variable rate
this system can make.

## The ET engine, and pyfao56

The narrative specifies pyfao56 for the dual crop coefficient balance, and that
is the right call. pyfao56 (Thorp, K. R., 2022, *SoftwareX* 19, 101208) comes out
of USDA-ARS, is peer reviewed, and is validated against Maricopa lysimeter
records, AZMET, and the University of Idaho Ref-ET software. An FAO-56
implementation nobody has checked against a standard is a liability however
carefully it was written.

So pyfao56 is a dependency and `pyfao56_bridge.py` makes it the engine of record.
The bridge translates the Delaware side — DEOS weather, Coastal Plain series,
Delmarva crop curves, the grower's irrigation log — into pyfao56's `Parameters`,
`Weather`, `Irrigation`, `SoilProfile` and `Update` classes, runs the model, and
converts the output back into the records the rest of the application already
consumes. `GEO4IRR_ET_ENGINE` selects `pyfao56`, `builtin`, or `auto` (the
default: pyfao56 when importable).

Assimilated data goes in through pyfao56's `Update` class rather than by patching
the model. That is the door pyfao56 provides for exactly this — Thorp et al. used
it to push sUAS fractional cover into Kcb — and it is where the Sentinel-2 NDVI
and probe corrections belong.

**Two things stay outside pyfao56, deliberately.** FAO-56 has no capillary rise
term, so pyfao56 does not model upflux from a shallow water table. On Fallsington
and Pocomoke that is a real omission in July, and a run over those soils returns
a warning saying so. And the nightly batch needs a stateless per-day step that
can be interleaved with the Bayesian filter, where pyfao56 runs a season at a
time into a DataFrame — so the built-in engine remains, and the browser runs the
same balance in JavaScript for offline use.

**They have been compared.** `tools/compare_engines.py` replays a season through
both and diffs it day by day:

| | agreement |
|---|---|
| Reference ETo | max difference **0.001 in/day** across the season, and 0.01% across a range of hot, cool, humid and windy days |
| Kcb curve | **identical** |
| TAW | diverges up to 0.8 in |
| Season ETc | within about 5% |

The ETo agreement is the meaningful result: two independent implementations of
ASCE standardized reference ET landing on the same number means the foundation is
sound. The TAW divergence is real and explainable — FAO-56 expands the root zone
in proportion to crop development, while the built-in engine ties root depth to
canopy cover. That was a deliberate choice, it propagates into depletion and
stress, and pyfao56's behaviour is the one to prefer when they disagree.

`POST /api/et/compare` runs the same comparison over real weather for a real
field and returns the statistics with plain-language notes about what any
divergence means.

None of this is validation against Delaware. Two models agreeing tells you
neither has an arithmetic bug; only lysimeter, atmometer and probe records tell
you whether the answer is right for Sussex County.

## Satellite consumptive water use

`backend/app/engine/metric.py`, `landsat.py`, `POST /api/metric/season`, and the
**Water used (METRIC)** panel in the browser.

This is a second, independent measurement of the same quantity the water
balance estimates, and it is worth being exact about how they differ. FAO-56
asks what the crop *would* use given the weather and a coefficient. METRIC
measures what *did* leave the field, from the surface temperature: water
leaving a surface carries heat with it, so a field transpiring freely runs cool
and one that has run out runs hot.

    Rn = G + H + LE                 so                LE = Rn - G - H

Net radiation in, ground heat flux and sensible heat flux out, and the latent
heat left over is the energy that went into evaporating water. LE is the
residual, which is why the other three have to be right.

### Why Landsat and not Sentinel-2

METRIC needs land surface temperature. Sentinel-2 carries no thermal band, so
it cannot do this at all, at any resolution. Landsat 8 and 9 carry TIRS, and
Collection 2 Level-2 ships a surface temperature product at 30 m with the
emissivity and atmospheric terms already applied by USGS. Sentinel-2 keeps its
place for the vegetation indices, where its five-day revisit beats Landsat's
sixteen badly. The two are complementary: NDVI says where the crop is
struggling, METRIC says how much water actually left.

Nothing here needs an account. Collection 2 Level-2 is open, Element 84 index
it at earth-search, and a public TiTiler crops a scene to a field boundary.

### Internalized calibration, and why the panel shows you the two pixels

The hard term is the sensible heat flux, which depends on a near-surface air
temperature difference nobody measures per pixel. METRIC gets around that by
picking two anchor pixels in each scene, a well-watered one and a dry one,
where ET is known by assumption. At those two the energy balance is closed
backwards to give the temperature difference, a line is fitted through them,
and every other pixel is read off that line.

The strength is that errors in the atmospheric correction, the emissivity and
the radiation terms hit the anchors and the field alike and largely cancel in
the ratio. The weakness is that the whole scene rests on two pixel choices. So
the Calibration tab sets out which two and why, and the map circles both. If
the cold anchor has landed on a farm pond or the hot one on a poultry house
roof, the scene is wrong, and on Delmarva, where fields sit beside towns, that
is a real risk rather than a hypothetical. The default anchor search is CIMEC
(Allen et al. 2013), whose albedo test exists precisely to keep water out of
the cold anchor.

### What it is for, and what it is not for

Landsat 8 and 9 together cross Delaware about every eight days, and the
Delmarva summer sky takes a share of those, so a season yields six to ten
usable scenes. That is enough for a seasonal total and for seeing which end of
a pivot ran short. It is not enough to schedule from, which is why the daily
FAO-56 balance runs alongside rather than being replaced. Nobody should decide
tonight's set from a picture taken last Tuesday.

Gaps between passes are filled by interpolating the reference ET fraction and
multiplying by each day's own reference ET, not by interpolating actual ET.
The difference matters more than the energy balance itself for a seasonal
total: clear days are not a random sample of the days in a Delaware July, and
carrying ETrF across the gap keeps the real weather in it. When no daily
reference ET is available the code falls back to interpolating ET directly and
says so in the warnings rather than quietly producing a worse number.

### Provenance, and the licence consequence

`metric.py` is a port of [pyMETRIC](https://github.com/hectornieto/pyMETRIC)
and the pyTSEB library under it, not an independent implementation. Both are
GPL-3.0, a port is a derivative work, and so Geo4Irr is GPL-3.0. See
`ATTRIBUTION.md` for what was ported function by function, the four places this
port deliberately departs from the reference code and why, and what the licence
does and does not commit you to.

Two of those departures are upstream defects rather than choices. pyMETRIC's
`pet_asce` adds net outgoing longwave to net shortwave where ASCE subtracts it,
which puts reference ET about a fifth high and, because METRIC calibrates
against reference ET, takes every actual ET with it. And in `METRIC()` the call
that fills the aerodynamic resistance sits inside an `else` branch, so with
`use_METRIC_resistance=True`, the default, the resistance is never assigned and
every sensible heat flux comes out NaN. Both are fixed here and both are pinned
by tests in `backend/tests/test_metric.py`.

### Not validated against Delaware either

Same caveat as the water balance, and it bears repeating because an ET map
looks more authoritative than it is. The energy balance closes on every pixel,
the three cover types separate in the right order, and irrigated corn lands at
0.27 in/day on a hot clear July day against the roughly 0.25 in/day the UD
extension manual gives for peak corn at R1. None of that is validation. A
Delaware eddy-covariance or lysimeter record, or a hindcast against Carvel,
is what would make these numbers defensible. Until then, treat a seasonal total
as good to perhaps 10 to 15 percent.

## The Bayesian part, in plain terms

The water balance is a model, and models drift. The filter in `bayesian.py`
carries three things forward — root zone depletion, a multiplicative bias on the
tabular crop coefficient, and the stress coefficient — and corrects them whenever
a measurement arrives:

- **Soil moisture probes** correct depletion directly.
- **Canopy cover or NDVI** (UAV, Sentinel-2, Planet) corrects the Kcb bias, so a
  field that closed canopy two weeks early gets credited for the extra water use.
- **Canopy temperature** (CWSI, DANS) corrects Ks, catching stress before the
  water balance predicts it.

Each observation is weighted by its measured error variance. A research-grade TDR
probe moves the state much harder than a factory-calibrated single-point
capacitance sensor, which is correct and matters: uncalibrated capacitance probes
in sandy soils with variable bulk EC are considerably worse than the spec sheet
claims. Readings more than four combined standard deviations from the model are
gated out as probable equipment problems rather than assimilated — a probe bumped
by a tillage pass should not move the recommendation.

Process noise grows every day the filter runs without observations. That is the
mechanism that makes a field with no sensors report honestly wide intervals
instead of false precision, and it is what makes the sensor-value argument
quantitative rather than a sales pitch.

## Deploying for a statewide user base

**On the 20,000 number.** Delaware has roughly 2,300 farms. Twenty thousand users
only works if the tool reaches past farm operators to crop consultants,
conservation district and NRCS staff, irrigation dealers, and the Maryland and
Virginia Eastern Shore. Design for that from day one: `field_delegations` in the
schema exists because a consultant managing 60 fields across 12 operations is a
different shape of user than a grower with 4 pivots, and retrofitting delegated
access is painful. Realistically, plan for a few hundred fields in season one and
build the acquisition path through Extension and the districts.

**Compute is not the problem.** The expensive work is the nightly recomputation
of every field against new observations and a fresh forecast: about 15 ms of
engine time per field, so 100,000 fields finish in minutes on a handful of
workers. Nothing here needs a GPU or a queue broker at this size. Two application
containers behind a load balancer and a managed Postgres instance will carry the
whole state.

**Be a good neighbour on upstream APIs.** Cache weather per station-day, not per
field. Forty DEOS stations means about 40 upstream calls a night instead of one
per field. `station_daily` in the schema is that cache. NWS gridpoint forecasts
should be cached per grid cell for the same reason.

**Delivery beats the dashboard.** Growers will not open a website at 5 a.m. every
day for a season. The thing that gets used is a text message: *"Home pivot: start
today, 0.8 in, about 11 hours."* Build the SMS path early and treat the web app as
where people go when the text says something surprising.

## Before this goes near a real field

1. **Get a DEOS data agreement.** The parser in `weather.py` is deliberately
   tolerant because the endpoint and parameter names have changed over the life of
   the network. Pin the agreed contract with the University of Delaware and then
   tighten the parser.
2. **Validate against Delaware data.** The engine is FAO-56 with Delmarva
   parameters, but it has not been checked against local lysimeter, atmometer, or
   probe records. Run a season of hindcasts against UD Carvel Research and
   Education Center data and the atmometer work from Georgetown, Harbeson and
   Seaford before anyone acts on it.
3. **Have the nitrogen module reviewed.** UD Cooperative Extension and the
   Nutrient Management Program should sign off on the credits, and the interface
   should keep saying what it says now: this is a decision aid, and an operation
   applying nutrients to 10 or more acres still needs a certified plan.
4. **Ground-truth the litter values.** The manure analyses in `nitrogen.py` are
   book values. Broiler litter varies enormously with bedding and house
   management. The interface asks for the grower's own analysis first, and it
   should stay that way.
5. **Pilot small.** Ten to fifteen cooperators across Sussex and Kent, one season,
   with probes installed and a gravimetric check. Compare what the tool said to
   what they did and what the crop did. Publish the misses.

## Known limits

- Nitrate leaching is piston displacement through the mobile water fraction, not
  a Richards-equation solute transport model. Directionally right, responsive to
  the variables a grower controls, and honest about not being more than that.
- SWAT+ multi-season integration is described in `advisory.py` and the schema but
  the coupling itself is not implemented here. It is the natural second phase.
- The frontend's offline mode uses climatological weather for the historical run.
  Real answers need the DEOS feed; the interface says so when it is running on
  normals.
- METRIC runs on the API only, not in the browser: nine band reads and a
  per-pixel stability iteration per scene is not work for a tab on a farm road.
  Every other panel works offline.
- The hourly reference ET at the overpass is approximated as 11 percent of the
  day's total when the station does not publish an hourly figure. It is the
  weakest input in the METRIC chain and it is named as such in the panel. A
  measured hourly value, or the ASCE hourly equation the engine falls back to
  when no station figure exists at all, are both better.
- Neither the water balance nor METRIC has been validated against a Delaware
  field record. Agreement between methods is not validation.
- Nothing here is a substitute for a probe and a shovel, particularly in the first
  season on a new field.
